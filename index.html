Foundation Knowledge

Engineering Fundamentals

Before you can qualify an engineer confidently, you need to understand the world they work in. This section explains how engineering projects work, where different engineers sit inside them, what equipment they design, and how to recognise the right profile on a call. No prior engineering knowledge required.

The Project Journey — From Idea to Built Substation

Imagine a grid operator like TenneT (Transmission System Operator) says: "We need a new substation. We have a location, a budget, and a deadline." That single statement kicks off a chain of work lasting three to five years and employing dozens of engineers. Here is how that chain works.

Stage 1 — The Idea (Concept)

Someone decides the substation needs to exist. They know what it must do — connect an offshore wind farm to the grid, step voltage down from 380kV to 110kV, handle a defined number of megawatts. At this stage there are no drawings and no engineers on site. There is a business case. A project is born.

Stage 2 — Is It Possible? (Feasibility)

A small team of senior engineers asks whether this can actually be built. What are the constraints — land, environment, grid capacity, budget? They produce rough calculations and sketches. The output is not engineering yet. It is a decision document: yes, this is feasible, here is roughly what it will cost and how long it will take.

Stage 3 — What Are We Building? (FEED — Front End Engineering Design)

This is where real engineers are deployed in meaningful numbers for the first time. FEED (Front End Engineering Design) turns the concept into a defined scope. By the end of FEED, everyone agrees on what is being built, at what voltage levels, with what equipment configuration, and within what cost envelope.

The Primary Engineer enters the project here. They produce the first real SLD (Single Line Diagram) showing how power will flow through the system. They write the Basis of Design — the document that sets all the rules everything downstream must follow. They do preliminary equipment sizing. By the end of FEED, the project is real and fundable.

Stage 4 — How Exactly Are We Building It? (Basic Design)

Basic design takes the FEED output and develops it into detailed engineering specifications ready for procurement. The Primary Engineer now produces fully detailed SLDs, writes complete equipment specifications, and conducts or reviews power system studies — load flow and short circuit analysis. These results determine what the equipment must be rated to withstand.

The Secondary Engineer enters the project here. They take the Primary Engineer's SLD and start designing the control world — how circuit breakers are operated, what protection philosophy will be applied, how signals flow from field devices back to the control room. By the end of Basic Design, major equipment orders are placed. Transformers and HV (High Voltage) switchgear have lead times of 12 to 18 months, so ordering must happen now while Detailed Design continues in parallel.

Stage 5 — Every Last Detail (Detailed Design)

The largest phase by headcount and duration. Detailed Design produces the complete construction package — every drawing, every cable, every wire, every calculation, every specification, and every test procedure. This is what physical construction teams build from.

The Primary Engineer is still active — finalising earthing calculations, reviewing vendor drawings as equipment arrives from manufacturers, resolving technical queries. Their peak was earlier. The Secondary Engineer is at full intensity — thousands of wiring diagrams, a cable schedule with hundreds or thousands of entries, panel layouts, terminal schedules, and I/O (Input/Output) list management.

FairPlay Engineering Focus

Our focus is FEED through Detailed Design — the design phases. Not Construction, not Commissioning, not Operations. The engineers we place are designing, not building or testing. When you qualify a candidate, you are listening for the language of design: documents, deliverables, specifications, studies, reviews, approvals. Not: installation, commissioning, site supervision, punch lists, handover.

Primary Engineer — Profile and Equipment
HV power circuit design · FEED to Detailed Design · The architect of the electrical system

The Primary Engineer designs the high voltage infrastructure through which electricity actually flows. They decide what equipment the substation uses, how it is configured, and what the power circuit looks like from end to end. Every other engineer on the project works from what the Primary Engineer defines.

Think of them as the architect of a building — they decide where every room goes and how big the power supply needs to be. The Secondary Engineer is the electrician who then wires everything inside those rooms.

How to recognise a real Primary Engineer on a call

They talk about documents they authored: "I produced the SLD for the 150kV substation" or "I wrote the transformer specification."

Use this question: "Tell me about the last SLD (Single Line Diagram) you produced from scratch. What voltage level was it? What bus configuration did you use and why was it chosen?" A real Primary Engineer will answer this confidently and specifically. A site engineer will struggle.

Primary Equipment — Visual Reference

The equipment a Primary Engineer specifies and designs around. Understanding what these look like helps you follow a candidate's explanation during a call.

Power Transformer
The most important single piece of equipment on the primary side

A power transformer steps voltage up or down between transmission levels — for example 380kV down to 150kV, or 150kV down to 33kV. The primary engineer specifies the MVA (Megavolt Ampere) rating, the impedance, the vector group, and the tap changer range. The OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer) allows the transformer to adjust voltage while energised. The Buchholz relay detects internal faults by measuring gas accumulation. Cooling radiators dissipate heat generated by electrical losses. Lead times for large power transformers are 12 to 18 months — which is why they are ordered during Basic Design.

AIS vs GIS Switchgear
The same electrical function — two completely different physical forms

Switchgear is the equipment that connects, disconnects, and interrupts the HV (High Voltage) circuit. AIS (Air Insulated Switchgear) is traditional outdoor equipment where air provides the electrical insulation between conductors. It requires significant space but is relatively simple to inspect and maintain. GIS (Gas Insulated Switchgear) encloses all the same components — circuit breakers, disconnectors, earthing switches, CTs (Current Transformers) — inside sealed metal housings filled with SF₆ (Sulphur Hexafluoride) gas, which is an excellent electrical insulator. GIS occupies roughly roughly ten times smaller the space of AIS, making it the specification choice for offshore platforms, compact urban substations, and any site where land is constrained or expensive.

Busbar Configurations
How feeders connect to a substation determines its reliability and flexibility

The busbar is the copper or aluminium conductor that connects multiple circuits together at the same voltage level. The configuration chosen determines how the substation behaves when equipment fails or requires maintenance. A single busbar is simple but means the entire substation shuts down if the busbar itself develops a fault. Double busbar is the most common configuration in transmission substations — any feeder can connect to either bus, allowing maintenance without losing supply. Ring bus and breaker-and-a-half configurations provide progressively higher reliability and are used in the most critical nodes of the transmission network. When a candidate says "I designed a double busbar 150kV substation" you now know exactly what that means.

Secondary Engineer — Profile and Equipment
Control and signal circuit design · Basic Design through Detailed Design · The nervous system engineer

The Secondary Engineer designs everything that controls, monitors, and interfaces with the primary HV equipment. While the primary circuit carries tens of thousands of volts, the secondary world operates at 110V DC (Direct Current, battery backed) or 230V AC (Alternating Current). Secondary Engineers design the wiring, control panels, cable systems, and signal interfaces that make the substation operable as a system.

The Secondary Engineer cannot start their work until the Primary Engineer has defined what equipment exists and what signals it generates. If the Primary Engineer changes the SLD — adds a feeder, changes a bus configuration — the Secondary Engineer may need to update hundreds of downstream documents.

How to recognise a real Secondary Engineer on a call

They talk in volumes and tool names: "I managed the cable schedule for a project with 800 cables." They name ELCAD, EPLAN P8, or MicroStation specifically and describe what they produced with them.

Use this question: "Describe the largest I/O (Input/Output) list you personally authored. How many signals were in it and what types of signals made up the majority?" A genuine Secondary Engineer gives a specific number and describes digital inputs, digital outputs, and analogue signals confidently. A vague answer usually means reviewer or site background.

Secondary Equipment — Visual Reference

The systems and equipment a Secondary Engineer designs, documents, and coordinates.

Relay Panel (Control Panel)
What a Secondary Engineer designs and a panel builder fabricates

A relay panel houses the protection relays, Bay Control Unit (BCU), metering equipment, control switches, and terminal blocks for a specific bay or section of the substation. The Secondary Engineer designs the panel layout — deciding what goes where and how it is wired — and produces the wiring diagrams and terminal schedules that the panel fabricator follows. Protection relays (such as the Siemens SIPROTEC 7SA87 shown) receive current and voltage measurements from the primary circuit and trip the circuit breaker if a fault is detected. The BCU (Bay Control Unit) collects status information and measurement data and communicates with the SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) system over the station network. A large substation may have 20 to 50 individual relay panels.

SCADA Architecture
How operators see and control the grid from a control room hundreds of kilometres away

SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) is the system that gives grid operators real time visibility and remote control of the entire network. In a modern IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) 61850 substation, data flows from field devices — transformers, circuit breakers, sensors — through Bay Control Units over a station LAN (Local Area Network) to a SCADA server, which presents live data to operator workstations. IEC 61850 GOOSE (Generic Object Oriented Substation Events) messages travel in under 4 milliseconds for protection tripping, replacing thousands of hardwired copper cables. PAC (Protection, Automation and Control) engineers design the SCADA architecture and configure the communication system. TenneT operates enterprise grade SCADA systems managing hundreds of substations across the Netherlands and Germany.

Cable Types and Tray Management
What the cable schedule documents and why segregation matters

Every cable in a substation has a unique number and is listed in the cable schedule — one of the most important Secondary Engineer deliverables. A large project has 500 to 2,000 cables. Each entry records: source location and terminal, destination location and terminal, cable type (XLPE, PILC, multicore control), conductor cross section in mm² (1.5mm² for control signals, 4–16mm² for power), voltage rating, screen earthing arrangement, route, and length. Cables are physically managed in ladder type cable trays mounted to walls and ceilings. Crucially, HV (High Voltage) power cables and LV (Low Voltage) control cables must be physically separated because HV cables generate electromagnetic interference that corrupts protection signals. The cable routing drawings produced by the Secondary Engineer show exactly how every cable reaches its destination and specify the segregation requirements.

How Primary and Secondary Connect

The simplest version of how these two roles depend on each other:

Primary Draws the SLD (Single Line Diagram) showing a 150kV circuit breaker between Busbar A and Transformer T1. Specifies that circuit breaker as 150kV, 3150A, 40kA fault rating. Issues the transformer datasheet and protection philosophy.
Secondary takes over ↓
Designs everything needed to operate that circuit breaker: the closing circuit, the tripping circuit, the position indicators, the interlocks that prevent dangerous switching, and the control cable running 200 metres from the switchyard back to the control room. Produces wiring diagram, panel layout, cable schedule entry, and terminal schedule for every connection.
PAC builds on both ↓ Uses the Primary SLD to understand fault currents, uses the Secondary wiring to know which terminals to connect to, and configures the protection relay to detect faults on that transformer and trip that circuit breaker in under 100 milliseconds via a GOOSE (Generic Object Oriented Substation Events) message over the station network.
PAC Engineer — Protection, Automation and Control
LV to MV signals · HV integration · The intelligence layer of the substation

The PAC engineer is the most specialised of the three electrical engineering profiles. They make the substation safe, observable, and automated. Safe means that if anything goes wrong, the right equipment disconnects in milliseconds. Observable means operators in a control room hundreds of kilometres away can see the real time state of the grid. Automated means switching sequences and safety interlocks run reliably without human error. This is the hardest profile to find in the market and commands the highest salaries of the three disciplines.

PAC engineers enter the project later than Primary engineers but work in parallel with Secondary engineers. They cannot start until the primary equipment is defined (so they know what to protect) and the secondary wiring is understood (so they know what terminals to connect to). On large projects these are distinct specialists. On smaller projects a single engineer may cover both Secondary and PAC.

What a PAC Engineer Designs

Protection Architecture

Which relays protect which assets, what principles are used (distance, differential, overcurrent), how protection zones are defined, what happens when primary protection fails. The output is a protection philosophy document and relay settings for every protection relay on the project. This is safety critical documentation subject to formal client review and witness testing.

IEC 61850 Communication

Modern substations communicate over Ethernet rather than hardwired copper. GOOSE (Generic Object Oriented Substation Events) messages carry protection trip signals in under 4 milliseconds. MMS carries SCADA data. The PAC engineer designs this communication architecture, configures the station LAN, and produces the SCL (Substation Configuration Language) files that describe the entire system.

SCADA Architecture

How the substation is monitored and controlled remotely. Data flows from field devices through Bay Control Units over a station LAN to a SCADA server, then over a Wide Area Network to the national control centre. The PAC engineer specifies what data points to collect, how alarms are structured, and how the operator interface is organised.

Automation Logic

Automatic functions that run without operator input — automatic bus transfer (switching supply to a healthy bus if the primary fails), automatic reclosing (reconnecting a line after a transient fault), interlocking sequences more complex than simple relay contacts. PAC engineers who also program PLCs (Programmable Logic Controllers) are rare and significantly more valuable.

Key Tools

DIGSI 5 — Siemens configuration tool for SIPROTEC 5 relays. The most widely deployed protection relay family in Europe. Used for protection settings, IEC 61850 configuration, and test documentation. Candidates with DIGSI 5 experience are immediately deployable on TenneT supply chain projects.
PCM600 — ABB / Hitachi Energy tool for REX, RET, REF, and RED relay families. Very common in projects where ABB relays were specified.
OMICRON CMC — Industry standard for protection relay testing. Injects test currents and voltages to verify relay operation. Candidates who reference OMICRON have hands on commissioning validation experience.
DIgSILENT PowerFactory and ETAP — Used for protection coordination studies, fault current calculations, and relay reach calculations. Shared with Primary engineers on smaller project teams.
How to recognise a real PAC engineer on a call

They name specific relay types and the tools used to configure them: "I configured the distance protection on the 380kV line using DIGSI 5 and verified with OMICRON."

Use this question: "Have you worked with GOOSE messaging? Can you describe what a GOOSE message does and give me an example of how you used it on a project?" This separates candidates who have worked on modern digital substations from those whose experience is entirely with older hardwired systems. A real PAC engineer will describe a specific trip signal or interlocking scenario.

Project Lifecycle

Every engineering project, whether it is a substation in South Africa, a wind farm in the North Sea, or a refinery in the Middle East, follows the same backbone. Understanding this is what separates a credible recruiter from someone who just sends CVs.

The Simple Version
How to explain it in 30 seconds

A country needs power. Or a plant needs upgrading. Or a grid needs expanding. At the start there is no design, no equipment, and no engineers on site. Just a problem that needs solving. Everything that follows is structured around solving that problem in a logical sequence.

The Seven Phases

Phase 1: Concept and Feasibility
The client asks whether this is worth building, whether they can afford it, and whether it will actually work. Costs are rough. Designs are conceptual. If the numbers do not stack up, the project dies here. Engineers are barely involved at this point.
Phase 2: Pre FEED and FEED
Now it becomes real. An EPC contractor is brought in and engineering begins across all disciplines simultaneously. Electrical, mechanical, piping, civil, and instrumentation teams all start working at the same time. High level decisions get made here: system architecture, equipment selection, load calculations. Nothing is construction ready yet.
Phase 3: Detailed Design
Every system becomes precise. Exact cable routes, wiring diagrams, panel layouts, terminal connections. This is what the construction team actually builds from. Designs start clashing here. Electrical routes conflict with piping, equipment sizes change mid design, and grid requirements shift. This is where experienced engineers earn their value.
Phase 4: Procurement
The project starts spending real money. Transformers, switchgear, cables, control systems, and structural steel all get ordered. Engineers review supplier designs and make sure what is being bought actually fits the design. Ordering the wrong transformer means months of delay and a very unhappy client. Mistakes here are expensive and hard to undo.
Phase 5: Construction
The project moves to site. Construction managers, supervisors, and site engineers build what was designed. Drawings are never perfect. Site conditions force changes. Rework happens regularly. This is where theory meets reality. Strong engineers adapt without making it someone else's problem.
Phase 6: Commissioning
Everything gets tested. Power is introduced and systems are checked one by one. Does the protection trip correctly? Does SCADA communicate? Does the transformer behave as expected? This phase is intense and fast paced. If something fails here, the whole project team feels it. Commissioning experience is one of the strongest differentiators you will find in a candidate.
Phase 7: Operations and Handover
Power flows. The project becomes as built. Operations take over. Engineers move on to the next project and the cycle starts again.
Real World Friction Know This
What actually happens on projects
  • Designs change mid project when grid operators shift their requirements
  • Cable routes clash with piping routes in the 3D model
  • Vendors deliver equipment late or deliver the wrong item altogether
  • Site conditions force redesigns that nobody budgeted for
  • Budget cuts remove scope after detailed design is already complete
  • Contractor and consultant teams disagree on approvals, which delays everything downstream
Why This Matters for Recruiting

Strong engineers do not just design cleanly in ideal conditions. They solve problems under pressure and communicate across teams when things go wrong. When you interview a candidate, the friction is what you want to hear about. That is how you know they have been through the real thing.

Ask about what went wrong. Ask how they handled a design change mid project. Ask what they would do differently next time. Those answers tell you more than the whole CV combined.

Target Market

Netherlands Market

The Netherlands is your placement market. Understanding how it works, who the key players are, and what Dutch clients specifically look for in international candidates will make every conversation you have more credible and more effective.

Why the Netherlands

The Netherlands sits at the centre of the European energy transition. It is home to one of the largest offshore wind development pipelines in the world, a major gas infrastructure network that is actively being repurposed, and a dense industrial base in the Rotterdam port area that generates constant engineering demand across all disciplines.

TenneT is the Dutch national grid operator and also operates in Germany as an independent entity within the same group. TenneT is responsible for the high voltage transmission network and is a major driver of grid expansion, substation upgrades, and offshore wind grid connection. Between 2025 and 2029, TenneT Netherlands plans to invest €43 billion in the Dutch onshore and offshore grid alone. That is not a typo. Most electrical engineers you place in the Netherlands will be working on projects that connect to or are commissioned against TenneT standards.

Key Sectors
  • Offshore wind, the fastest growing sector in the Netherlands right now
  • Grid expansion and substation upgrades driven by large scale electrification
  • Oil and gas infrastructure in and around Rotterdam
  • Industrial and chemical processing (BASF, Shell, LyondellBasell)
  • Hydrogen infrastructure development at scale
  • Data centre electrical and mechanical build out
Working Language

English is the working language on almost every international engineering project in the Netherlands. Dutch is not a hard requirement for most roles. That said, candidates who show willingness to learn Dutch are viewed more positively by clients, particularly for longer term or permanent positions.

For short term contract roles in EPC environments, strong English fluency is all that is required.

HVDC vs HVAC

Most offshore projects under 100km from shore use HVAC for export cables. Projects further out, including IJmuiden Ver, are moving to HVDC because it is more efficient over long distances. HVDC requires converter stations at each end and uses fundamentally different equipment and design principles. Engineers with HVDC experience are rare and very sought after. If a candidate mentions HVDC, ask what their specific role was, which converter technology they worked with, and whether they were in design or commissioning.

Recruiter Note

Many strong electrical engineers have no offshore background. This does not disqualify them. Most offshore wind clients will accept candidates who understand HV substations deeply, because the electrical principles are the same. The marine environment is learnable. The fundamentals are not.

That is what clients respect in international hires.

Work Permits and Right to Work
Qualify this on every call with a non EU candidate. Do not skip it.

This is a fundamental qualification point. Before investing time in a candidate, you need to understand their right to work status. Do not assume and do not move forward without asking directly.

EU and EEA Citizens No Permit Required

Citizens of EU member states and EEA countries (Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) can live and work in the Netherlands without a permit. Post Brexit, UK citizens are no longer in this category and require a work permit.

Non EU Citizens Permit Required

Candidates from South Africa, the UK, the Middle East, and other non EU countries require a permit. The primary route for skilled engineers is the Highly Skilled Migrant visa (Kennismigrant). Only employers registered with the IND as recognised sponsors can apply on behalf of a candidate.

The 30% Ruling — A Major Relocation Incentive
Many candidates do not know this exists. It can change the entire relocation conversation.

The 30% ruling is a Dutch tax incentive that allows employers to pay 30% of a qualifying candidate's gross salary tax free as compensation for the costs of relocating from abroad. For a candidate earning €80,000 per year, this significantly reduces their effective tax burden and produces a materially higher take home amount than the headline Dutch income tax rate would suggest.

  • To qualify, the candidate must have lived more than 150 km from the Dutch border for at least 16 of the 24 months before starting work in the Netherlands.
  • South African, Middle Eastern, and most non European candidates qualify comfortably on this criterion.
  • The ruling applies for a maximum of 5 years from the start date.
  • From 2027, the ruling reduces to 27% for new applicants who first qualified in 2025 or later. Existing holders have transition arrangements until 2027.
  • The employer applies to the Dutch Tax Authority (Belastingdienst). The application must be submitted within 4 months of the candidate's start date.
  • Use this proactively in relocation conversations. It directly addresses the concern that Dutch taxes are very high.

Core Discipline

Electrical Engineering

Your primary discipline. Substations, high voltage, grid connection, offshore wind, oil and gas. This is where you need to be sharpest. Understanding the split between primary, secondary, protection, and SCADA is what separates a credible technical recruiter from one who is guessing their way through a call.

The Big Picture

Electrical engineers in this world are responsible for one thing: getting power from A to B safely, reliably, and controllably. In substations and grid projects, this splits into two distinct worlds. Primary deals with the physical equipment electricity flows through. Secondary deals with the intelligence that monitors, controls, and protects it. They cannot exist without each other, and strong candidates understand both even when they specialise in one.

Primary Physical

The muscle. Heavy equipment. Physical flow of electricity. Transformers, switchgear, busbars, cables. If it is tangible and carries current, it is primary.

Secondary Control

The nervous system. Protection relays, SCADA, control panels, signal wiring. It detects faults and tells the primary side when to shut down.

Critical Insight

Primary installs the transformer. Secondary decides when to shut it down if something fails. If secondary is wrong, equipment gets damaged. If primary is wrong, the system physically fails. They are equal in importance.

Engineering Discipline Profiles

Electrical engineering in substation and grid projects divides into three distinct disciplines. Each has its own deliverables, tools, and candidate profile. A recruiter who understands the difference can qualify faster, ask better questions, and avoid placing the wrong engineer in the wrong role.

Primary Engineering
High Voltage Systems · 10 kV to 400+ kV · The power circuit itself

Primary engineering covers the high voltage infrastructure through which electricity actually flows. These engineers design the physical and electrical architecture of substations. They decide which transformers to specify, how to configure the busbar system, where to position the switchgear, and how to design the earthing grid that keeps the site safe. Their output is the master documentation set from which everything else is built.

What They Design

Transformer Systems

Power transformers (step up and step down), autotransformers for interconnection between voltage levels, instrument transformers including current transformers (CTs) for protection and metering, and voltage transformers or capacitive voltage transformers (CVTs). Primary engineers specify tap changer requirements, impedance values, vector groups, and cooling systems.

Switchgear and Bus Architecture

AIS (Air Insulated Switchgear) is traditional outdoor equipment. GIS (Gas Insulated Switchgear) is compact, enclosed, and increasingly specified for offshore platforms and constrained urban sites. Bus configurations include single busbar (simple, less redundant), double busbar (most common in transmission), ring bus, and breaker and a half for the highest reliability requirements.

Earthing and Grounding Systems

The earthing grid protects people and equipment during fault conditions. Primary engineers calculate touch voltage and step voltage limits, design the grid conductor layout, specify earth electrodes, and verify compliance with IEC 61936. This is safety critical work and requires specialist calculation tools beyond standard CAD.

Reactive Power and Power Quality

Grid connected substations must manage reactive power to maintain stable voltages across the network. Primary engineers specify capacitor banks, reactors, SVCs (Static VAr Compensators), and STATCOMs. These are particularly relevant for offshore wind projects where long HVAC cable connections generate significant reactive power that must be compensated.

Key Deliverables

Single line diagrams showing the complete electrical topology, substation layout drawings for equipment positioning (plan and elevation views), equipment specification sheets and datasheets, earthing system design calculations, cable routing and sizing studies for HV connections, load flow study inputs, short circuit study inputs, technical bid evaluations for major plant procurement, and basis of design documents at FEED stage.

Tools in Depth

AutoCAD and AutoCAD Electrical — 2D drafting for SLDs, layout drawings, and general arrangement plans. Most primary engineers have AutoCAD as a baseline but ask specifically whether they produced SLDs or only layout work. SLD authorship indicates real design responsibility.
DIgSILENT PowerFactory — The dominant power system analysis tool in European utility and TSO environments. TenneT and all major European grid operators use PowerFactory for load flow, short circuit, dynamic stability, and harmonic studies. This is the tool to probe for in Netherlands market candidates. ETAP is more common in the Middle East and Americas but less so in European TSO supply chains.
ETAP (Electrical Transient Analysis Program) — Comprehensive power system modelling for load flow, short circuit, arc flash, and motor starting studies. Widely used in industrial, petrochemical, and offshore projects. Often found alongside PowerFactory in engineering consultancies that serve both utility and industrial clients.
CDEGS and WinIGS — Specialist grounding calculation tools. CDEGS (Current Distribution, Electromagnetic Fields, Grounding and Soil Structure Analysis) by SES Technologies is the industry standard for earthing grid design. If a primary engineer has used CDEGS, they have done serious earthing work. It is a significant differentiator.
PSCAD — Used for electromagnetic transient simulations and HVDC studies. More specialized than PowerFactory, relevant for candidates with offshore wind or HVDC transmission experience. Increasingly important as TenneT's programme expands into HVDC interconnectors.

IEC Standards to Probe

IEC 61936 covers power installations exceeding 1 kV AC and is the primary design standard for HV substations. IEC 60909 governs short circuit calculations. IEC 60071 covers insulation coordination. IEC 60287 covers cable current ratings. Candidates should be able to describe how they have applied these in practice, not just name them.

Secondary Engineering
Control and Signal Circuits · LV to MV · The nervous system of the substation

Secondary engineering is everything that controls, monitors, and interfaces with the primary high voltage equipment. While the primary circuit carries tens of thousands of volts, the secondary world operates at 110V DC (battery backed) or 230V AC. Secondary engineers design the wiring, control panels, cable systems, and signal interfaces that make the substation functional as an operated system. Their work is less visible but without it, the primary equipment cannot be controlled or protected.

What They Design

Wiring Diagrams and Schematics

Wiring diagrams show the exact connection of every wire in a panel, including wire numbers, cross sections, colours, source terminal, and destination terminal. Schematic drawings show the functional logic of control circuits: how does a circuit breaker close? What sequence of contacts must be satisfied? These are installation documents — tradespeople follow them wire by wire.

Cable Schedules and Routing

The cable schedule is a master list of every cable in the project. Each entry has a unique cable number, from and to location, cable type (XLPE, PILC, multicore control), cross section (typically 1.5mm to 16mm for control cables), voltage rating, screen earthing arrangement, length, and route. Cable routing drawings show the physical paths cables take through cable trays, conduits, and underground ducts. These are often the most laborious deliverables in secondary engineering.

I/O Lists and Interlocking Logic

The I/O (Input/Output) list is a comprehensive register of every signal between field equipment and the control system. Each signal has a tag number, type (digital input, digital output, analog 4 to 20 mA, PT100 temperature, pulse), description, source, and destination. Interlocking logic defines the safety sequences: a circuit breaker may only close if the isolators on both sides are already open. These prevent dangerous operations and are a critical safety deliverable.

Control Panel Design

Control panels house the relays, terminal blocks, switches, indicators, and interfaces that operate the substation. Secondary engineers design the physical layout of equipment within panels, specifying positions, mounting arrangements, thermal loads, and access requirements. Panel layouts are sent to panel builders for fabrication. The quality of this work directly affects installation time and commissioning ease.

Tertiary Engineering — What It Means

Some secondary engineers also cover tertiary systems, which are the auxiliary power supplies for the substation itself. UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply) systems keep control equipment running during grid faults. Battery backed DC systems at 110V or 48V supply protection relays and control circuits that must remain live regardless of grid conditions. BTU (Battery Terminal Units) manage the health and charging of these critical batteries. In smaller projects this work is absorbed by the secondary engineer.

Engineers who know ELCAD at project delivery level (not just viewing) have genuine secondary engineering depth. Common in German and Dutch EPC firms.
EPLAN Electric P8 — The most widely adopted electrical schematic software in European manufacturing and project engineering environments. Cable sizing calculates the minimum conductor cross section for a given load, voltage drop limit, and fault protection requirement. These questions separate production level secondary engineers from those who reviewed or updated others work.

PAC Engineering — Protection, Automation and Control
LV to MV Signals · HV Integration · The intelligence layer of the substation

PAC engineering sits at the intersection of secondary engineering and power systems. PAC engineers are responsible for making the substation safe, observable, and automated. Safe: if a fault occurs, the right equipment disconnects instantly. Observable: operators see the real time state of the grid via SCADA. Automated: switching sequences, interlocking, and automatic restoration run without manual intervention. This is the most technically demanding of the three electrical disciplines and the hardest profile to find in the market.

Protection Engineering

Protection is the system that isolates faults. When a short circuit occurs, currents can be tens of thousands of amperes within milliseconds. Protection relays detect abnormal conditions and send trip commands to circuit breakers before equipment is damaged. PAC engineers design the protection architecture: which relays protect which assets, how they communicate, and what their settings are.

Protection Types

Distance protection measures the impedance to the fault and trips if it falls within a defined zone. Used primarily on transmission lines. Differential protection compares current entering and leaving a protected zone: any difference indicates a fault inside. Used for transformers, busbars, and cables. Overcurrent protection is the simplest form: if current exceeds a threshold for long enough, it trips. Used as backup throughout the system.

Selectivity and Coordination

When a fault occurs, only the minimum necessary equipment should trip to preserve as much of the network as possible. Protection coordination studies model the full system and calculate relay settings that achieve this selectivity. The PAC engineer uses ETAP or PowerFactory to run these studies, then produces a relay settings document for each protection relay in the project. This document is safety critical and subject to formal review.

IEC 61850 — The Communication Standard for Modern Substations

IEC 61850 is the international standard defining how substation automation systems communicate. It has fundamentally changed how protection and control systems are designed. Understanding it is now essential for any PAC engineer working on new build projects in Europe.

GOOSE Messaging (Generic Object Oriented Substation Events) — High speed binary signals transmitted over Ethernet between protection relays and control units. A GOOSE message can communicate a trip command in under 4 milliseconds, replacing the hardwired trip circuits that secondary engineers traditionally designed. GOOSE has dramatically reduced wiring complexity in modern substations.
Sampled Values — Digital representation of current and voltage measurements transmitted from merging units (connected to instrument transformers) directly to protection relays over the process bus. Replaces the copper wiring from CTs and VTs to relays. Very modern installations only. Candidates with Sampled Values experience are at the leading edge of the technology.
MMS (Manufacturing Message Specification) — The protocol for SCADA communication within an IEC 61850 substation. Station level communication between bay control units and the substation SCADA server uses MMS over TCP/IP.
SCL Files (Substation Configuration Language) — XML based files used to configure IEC 61850 systems. The SSD (System Specification Description) captures functional requirements. The SCD (Substation Configuration Description) captures the complete configured system. Engineers who have worked with SCL files at a production level have genuine IEC 61850 depth.

SCADA Architecture

SCADA allows operators to monitor and control the grid from a central control room. In a modern IEC 61850 substation, data flows from bay control units over a station LAN to SCADA servers, which present real time data to operator workstations. PAC engineers design the SCADA architecture: what data points to collect, how to structure the SCADA database, what alarm priorities to set, and how the operator interface should be organised. TenneT operates enterprise grade SCADA systems managing hundreds of substations across the Netherlands and Germany.

Tools in Depth

DIGSI 5 — Siemens configuration and engineering tool for SIPROTEC 5 protection relays. The most widely deployed protection relay family in European substations. PAC engineers use DIGSI to set protection functions, configure IEC 61850 communication, and generate test documentation. Candidates with DIGSI 5 experience are immediately deployable on TenneT supply chain projects.
SFT2841 and PCM600 — ABB (now Hitachi Energy) tools for configuring REX, RET, REF, and RED protection relay families. SFT2841 is the older tool for legacy relays. PCM600 (Protection and Control IED Manager) is the current generation, supporting IEC 61850. Very common in projects where ABB relays were specified.
Siemens PLC (S7 Series) — Programmable Logic Controllers for automation sequences: automatic bus transfer, interlocking beyond simple relay logic, sequential switching programmes. PAC engineers who also programme PLCs are rare and valuable — they bridge the gap between protection and process automation.
OMICRON CMC — The industry standard test equipment for protection relay testing. Used during commissioning to inject test currents and voltages and verify relay operation. PAC engineers who reference OMICRON testing have hands on relay commissioning experience — valuable context even for office design roles.
ETAP and DIgSILENT PowerFactory — Used for protection coordination studies. The PAC engineer uses the same power system model as the primary engineer but focuses on fault current magnitudes, relay reach calculations, and time grading curves. Shared tool usage between primary and PAC disciplines is common in leaner project teams.

Knowing the difference and being able to ask for it is what keeps you from presenting the wrong person to the wrong client.

Mechanical

Focus: equipment. Pumps, compressors, turbines, heat exchangers, and vessels. Either rotating, which means it moves, or static, which means it does not.

Piping

Focus: flow. The network of pipes, valves, and fittings that connect mechanical equipment. Piping engineers design routing and ensure safety under pressure and temperature.

Where They Fit on a Project

In oil and gas and petrochemical projects, mechanical and piping are often the largest engineering disciplines by headcount. In power and substation projects they are smaller but still present, for example in transformer cooling systems, HVAC for offshore platforms, and fire protection systems.

  • Gas compressors (centrifugal, reciprocating, screw)
  • Steam and gas turbines
  • Fans and blowers

  • Condition monitoring systems

"Walk me through how you specified a centrifugal pump for a project." A strong answer covers duty conditions, the relevant API standard, vendor alignment meetings, and any technical deviations raised during the review process.

Static Equipment Engineer
Pressure vessels, tanks, heat exchangers. Design codes are everything here.

What They Work On

  • Pressure vessels and reactors
  • Storage tanks (atmospheric and pressurised)
  • Shell and tube heat exchangers
  • Air cooled heat exchangers
  • Columns and towers
  • Fired heaters and furnaces

Standards They Should Know

  • ASME VIII for pressure vessel design (US standard, common in Middle East projects)
  • PED (Pressure Equipment Directive) for EU projects including the Netherlands
  • API 650 and 620 for tanks
  • TEMA for heat exchangers
  • EN 13445 for European unfired pressure vessels
Netherlands Note

Candidates from the Middle East will likely know ASME VIII. For Netherlands placement, PED is the relevant standard. Ask whether they have worked to PED before and whether they understand the key differences. Most experienced static equipment engineers can adapt, but it is worth confirming upfront.

Piping Engineer
Routing, layout, and stress. A standalone discipline, not a subset of mechanical.

Piping engineers are often confused with mechanical engineers but have a distinct skillset. They design the systems that connect equipment and ensure safe, efficient flow under real operating conditions including pressure, temperature, and thermal expansion forces.

What They Work On

  • P&IDs, the map of the entire plant
  • Piping layouts and routing in 3D models (PDMS, E3D, SP3D)
  • Isometric drawings for fabrication
  • Pipe stress analysis using Caesar II
  • Material selection and pipe class specifications
  • Valve selection and specification

Key Differentiators

  • 3D modelling experience in PDMS, E3D, or SP3D is highly marketable
  • Stress analysis using Caesar II is a specialist skill
  • P&ID development is a senior level competency
  • Pipe class development is also senior and not all piping engineers do this
How to Qualify Mechanical and Piping Engineers

"Are you more on the rotating side, static side, or piping? And within that, are you more in design and specification, or vendor management and site?"

Most experienced engineers identify their niche without hesitation. Junior engineers tend to claim all of them equally. Push back gently and ask which one they have spent the most time on in the last two years.

"What software do you use for your design work?"

Piping engineers should mention PDMS, E3D, SP3D, or Caesar II. Mechanical engineers on vessels should mention COMPRESS or PV Elite. Heat exchanger work should come with HTRI. Not knowing specific tools for their claimed speciality is a red flag worth exploring.

"Have you worked with vendors? What does that process look like for you?"

Strong mechanical engineers have been through vendor evaluation. They have written specifications, received bids, done technical evaluations, attended FATs, and raised technical deviations. A candidate who has never interacted with vendors has been in a purely execution or support role.

Discipline

Civil & Structural

Without civil and structural engineers nothing stands. They design the ground everything sits on and the steel and concrete that holds everything up. Often underestimated in recruitment, but every major project runs through them and no other discipline can start building until they have delivered their scope.

Civil Engineering
The ground and what sits below it
  • Foundation design (spread footings, piled foundations)
  • Site grading, drainage, and earthworks
  • Roads and hardstandings
  • Underground cable and pipe route design
  • Civil drawings and specifications
  • Geotechnical interpretation and coordination
Structural Engineering
Everything above the ground
  • Steel frame design (buildings, pipe racks, gantries)
  • Concrete structures (buildings, sumps, bunds)
  • Equipment support frames and plinths
  • Structural analysis and load calculations
  • Connection design (bolted and welded)
  • Seismic and wind load analysis
Where They Fit on a Project

Civil and structural engineers are involved from concept through to the end of construction. They are among the first disciplines to engage on a project because you cannot design anything else until you understand the ground conditions and the structural envelope. They are also among the last to finish because structures need to be complete before equipment can be installed and commissioned.

How to Qualify Civil and Structural Engineers

"Is your focus more civil (foundations, earthworks, drainage) or structural (steel and concrete above ground)?"

The two overlap but most engineers have a primary area. Structural engineers typically have more analytical depth including structural calculations and finite element analysis. Civil engineers typically have more site exposure and coordination experience.

"What analysis software do you use?"

Structural engineers should mention STAAD.Pro, ETABS, SAP2000, or Tekla. Civil engineers should mention AutoCAD Civil 3D or Civil Designer. A candidate who cannot name a structural analysis tool is likely in a predominantly drafting role.

"What codes and standards do you design to?"

For Netherlands work, the relevant standard is the Eurocode suite (EC2 for concrete, EC3 for steel). Candidates from South Africa know SANS codes. Candidates from the Middle East may know AISC or BS codes. The ability and willingness to adapt to Eurocodes is the key point to qualify.

Discipline

Cost & Project Controls

These roles exist to make sure projects finish on time and within budget. They are not engineers in the technical sense but they are essential on every large project. On mega projects, cost and controls teams can be as large as the entire engineering team.

Cost Engineer
What things cost and when
  • Estimate project costs at each phase (Class 5 through Class 1)
  • Develop cost breakdown structures
  • Track actual versus budgeted spend
  • Manage change orders and variation claims
  • Produce cost reports for project management
  • Forecast cost at completion
Planner and Scheduler
When things happen
  • Develop project schedules in Primavera P6 or MS Project
  • Set up Work Breakdown Structures
  • Track progress against the baseline schedule
  • Identify critical path and schedule risk
  • Produce weekly and monthly progress reports
  • Coordinate schedules across all disciplines
Project Controls
Cost and schedule combined into overall project performance
  • Earned value management (EVM)
  • Risk registers and risk management
  • Document control
  • Interface management on large and complex projects
  • KPI reporting and performance dashboards
How to Qualify

"What scheduling tools do you use? Primavera P6 specifically?"

P6 is the industry standard for large EPC projects. Candidates without P6 should at minimum have strong earned value knowledge and a clear plan to upskill.

"Have you worked on the EPC side or the owner side?"

EPC side controls is faster paced and more pressured. Owner side is more focused on monitoring and governance. Both are valid but the experience profile is quite different, and the client will usually have a preference.

Recruiter Tool

Call Framework

Run every call the same way. This is your 20 to 30 minute structure. The goal is not to follow a script. It is to make sure you come out of every call knowing exactly what the candidate does, how good they are at it, and whether they are worth moving forward.

Before You Start

Have the job spec open. Know which discipline you are hiring for. Have your notes open. The call should feel natural. You are guiding a conversation, not running an interrogation.

0 to 2 min
Set the Frame
▼

Goal: control the call early. Candidates who talk without direction waste your time and theirs. Set the expectation upfront so you can redirect confidently if needed.

"I will go through your background in a structured way, we will cover your project experience in detail, and then I will explain the opportunity and we will see if it makes sense. Does that work?"

This does two things. It tells the candidate you are structured, and it gives you permission to redirect if they go off track.

2 to 8 min
Career Snapshot
▼

Goal: understand their career arc quickly. Industries, disciplines, years of experience. You are filtering here, not going deep yet.

"Give me a quick overview of your experience. What kind of projects, what discipline, and where you have been based."

Listen for project types, discipline clarity, and whether their career shows logical progression. This phase should take no more than five or six minutes.

Strong at this stage

  • Clear industry exposure with project types named
  • Can describe their discipline and specialisation precisely
  • Career shows logical progression

Red flags at this stage

  • Rambling with no clear structure
  • No clear project exposure, only "various projects"
  • Cannot define their own role or discipline
8 to 18 min
Project Deep Dive
▼

Goal: understand real experience versus surface knowledge. This is where most recruiters fail because they accept general answers. Push here.

"Walk me through one project you worked on from start to finish. What was the project, what was your role, and what did you personally own?"

Layer 1: Ownership

"What were you personally responsible for on that project?"

You want personal ownership. Not "the team did X" but "I was responsible for X". If everything is described as a team effort with no individual contribution named, they were likely in a support role.

Layer 2: Technical Depth (select based on discipline)

Electrical Primary:

"What voltage levels did you work at and what equipment were you specifying or designing around?"

Electrical Secondary or Protection:

"What protection schemes were you working on and were you involved in the settings or just the drawings?"

SCADA:

"What communication protocols were you working with and were you designing the integration or maintaining an existing system?"

Instrumentation and Control:

"Were you working on PLC or DCS logic, or more on the field instrumentation side? Which platform?"

Mechanical:

"What type of equipment, rotating or static, and were you in specification, vendor management, or site based work?"

Layer 3: Project Phase

"Which phases were you involved in? FEED, detailed design, construction, or commissioning?"

Commissioning exposure is a major differentiator. Engineers who have been on site during energisation understand real failure modes. This makes them considerably more valuable than office only candidates.

Layer 4: The Problem Test

"What went wrong on that project and how did you deal with it?"

This is the most important question on the call. Weak candidates say nothing went wrong. Strong candidates have a specific story with a clear resolution and a lesson taken from it.

18 to 23 min
Validation
▼

Goal: test consistency and depth of thinking, not just memory recall.

"If you had to do that project again, what would you do differently?"

"What part of your work had the biggest impact on the project outcome?"

You are looking for self awareness and genuine engagement with their own experience. Candidates who cannot answer these questions honestly have not reflected on their own work.

23 to 27 min
Motivation and Relocation
▼

Goal: qualify their willingness to relocate to the Netherlands and understand what they are actually looking for in their next move.

"What are you looking for in your next move? Project type, location, contract versus permanent?"

"Have you lived or worked outside your home country before? How did you find that experience?"

"Are you open to relocating to the Netherlands? Do you have family considerations we should be aware of at this stage?"

International experience is a positive signal. Engineers who have already lived in a different country adapt to new environments much faster. A candidate who has never left their home country is not disqualified, but the relocation conversation needs more time and depth.

"What are your salary expectations and do you have a sense of Dutch market rates in your discipline?"

Many candidates from South Africa or the Middle East have expectations that are either too high or too low. Set context without making commitments. Do not quote specific figures without checking internally first.

"Do you currently hold EU residency or citizenship, or would you need a work permit for the Netherlands?"

This is non negotiable. Do not progress a candidate without understanding their right to work status. The Highly Skilled Migrant visa requires employer sponsorship and takes time to process. Set realistic expectations early.

27 to 30 min
Close and Next Steps
▼

Goal: set clear next steps and maintain control of the process. Never leave a call without a specific action on both sides.

Explain the role clearly: project type, phase, discipline focus, and approximate duration. Set a specific timeline for your next contact.

"Based on what we have discussed, I think this is worth exploring further. I will review your profile against the brief and come back to you by [specific date]. Does that work for you?"

Do not say you will be in touch without a date. That is not a next step. A next step has a date attached to it.

CV Red Flag Guide

    
How to read an engineering CV in ten minutes. Most CVs are written to impress, not to inform. Your job is to cut through the language and understand what the person actually did on each project versus what they observed, reviewed, or supported.


    

      
The Ten Minute Scan — What to Look For First

      
Before reading a word of the content, look at the structure. Strong engineering CVs share the same pattern. Weak ones share a different pattern. You can filter roughly 40 percent of CVs on structure alone before reading a single project description.

      

        

          
Strong CV Pattern

          
    
            
  • Project descriptions name specific deliverables: SLDs, cable schedules, relay settings, specifications
    • 
            
  • Software tools named with context: "used ETAP for short circuit studies" not just "ETAP"
    • 
            
  • Voltage levels stated: 150kV, 33kV, 11kV — not just "HV"
    • 
            
  • Progression visible: more responsibility each role
    • 
            
  • Project phases mentioned: FEED, Basic Design, Detailed Design
    • 
            
  • Client or end user named: TenneT, Eskom, Shell, BP
    • 
            
  • IEC standard numbers cited where relevant
    • 
          

        

        

          
Weak CV Pattern

          
    
            
  • Responsibilities listed as generic bullet points: "responsible for electrical design"
    • 
            
  • Software tools listed as a block at the top with no context
    • 
            
  • Voltage levels absent or vague: "HV installations" with no kV figure
    • 
            
  • Every role has the same responsibility bullets regardless of seniority
    • 
            
  • No project phases mentioned anywhere on the CV
    • 
            
  • No client names — just contractor company names
    • 
            
  • Heavy use of passive voice: "involved in", "assisted with", "exposure to"
    • 
          

        

      

    


    

      
Design vs Site — The Most Important Distinction

      
FairPlay places office based design engineers. The market is full of highly experienced engineers who have spent their careers on site commissioning, installing, testing, and operating systems that other engineers designed. Their CVs can look almost identical to a design engineer's CV. Here is how to tell them apart.

      

        

          
Design Engineer Language

          
    
            
  • "I produced the SLD for..."
    • 
            
  • "I authored the cable schedule..."
    • 
            
  • "I specified the 150kV transformer..."
    • 
            
  • "I ran the load flow studies..."
    • 
            
  • "I designed the earthing grid..."
    • 
            
  • "I wrote the protection philosophy..."
    • 
            
  • "Issued for Construction", "Issued for Review"
    • 
            
  • "FEED stage", "Basic Design", "Detailed Design"
    • 
          

        

        

          
Site Engineer Language

          
    
            
  • "I commissioned the...", "I tested the..."
    • 
            
  • "I installed and terminated..."
    • 
            
  • "Site supervisor for..."
    • 
            
  • "Punch list management"
    • 
            
  • "FAT and SAT" as their primary activity
    • 
            
  • "On site during construction"
    • 
            
  • "Loop checking", "cable pulling", "glanding"
    • 
            
  • "Handover", "mechanical completion"
    • 
          

        

      

      

        
The Grey Zone

        
Some engineers genuinely do both. A strong senior engineer at a smaller EPC firm may design during the week and visit site occasionally to check their own work. The key question is: what percentage of their time was office design versus site? Ask directly: "On a typical month, how much of your time was in the office producing documents versus on site?" A design engineer will say 80 to 100 percent office. A site engineer will say 50 percent or more on site.

      

    


    

      
Seniority Red Flags

      
Years of experience alone does not determine seniority. A candidate with 12 years in a junior role is not a senior engineer. Look for evidence of increasing responsibility, not just increasing tenure.

      

        
Claims senior with fewer than 7 years total experience. Senior in most EPC and consulting firms requires 8 to 12 years minimum. Under 7 years and calling themselves senior is an immediate flag to probe.

        
No evidence of leading or owning a scope. A senior engineer should be able to describe a project where they were the lead for a discipline scope. If every role description is about contributing to someone else's scope, they are not senior.

        
Same bullet points from junior to senior roles. If the responsibilities look identical across every job regardless of title, the progression is in name only. Real career growth shows expanding scope and complexity.

        
No mention of reviewing or checking others' work. From mid-level upward, engineers review junior work. If there is no mention of checking, reviewing, or technically approving anything, they have likely not progressed beyond execution.

        
Promotion every 12 to 18 months at one company. Fast internal promotion can be genuine or can indicate title inflation. Ask what actually changed between roles. If the answer is "the title and pay", the seniority may not be real.

      

    


    

      
Tool and Software Red Flags

      
Engineers often list every tool they have ever opened. The question is not whether they have used something but at what level and for what purpose.

      

        

          
Red Flag Tool Patterns

          
    
            
  • Lists 15 or more tools with no context
    • 
            
  • Lists AutoCAD but no CAD deliverables in project descriptions
    • 
            
  • Lists ETAP or PowerFactory but no studies mentioned anywhere
    • 
            
  • Lists DIGSI or PCM600 but no relay configuration work described
    • 
            
  • Mentions EPLAN but every role is described as "checking" not "producing"
    • 
          

        

        

          
Strong Tool Evidence

          
    
            
  • Tool named and linked to a specific output: "produced SLDs in AutoCAD"
    • 
            
  • Study results mentioned: "ran short circuit analysis in ETAP, rated equipment to 40kA"
    • 
            
  • Version specifics where relevant: "DIGSI 5 for SIPROTEC 5 configuration"
    • 
            
  • Tool used at production level, not viewer level
    • 
            
  • Tool matches the seniority claim
    • 
          

        

      

    


    

      
The One Question That Catches Everything

      
After reviewing a CV, ask this before anything else:

      

        
"Walk me through the last document you produced from scratch. What was it, what software did you use, and who reviewed it before it was issued?"

      

      
A design engineer answers this immediately and specifically. A site engineer hesitates, names something vague, or describes something someone else produced that they used on site. The reviewer question is important — documents that go through a formal review process are real design deliverables. Documents with no review process are usually informal work products.

Sample Answers

    
What strong answers actually sound like versus weak ones. Use these during calls to calibrate quickly. You do not need to hear the exact words — you are listening for the pattern. Strong answers are specific, confident, and linked to real deliverables. Weak answers are vague, passive, and describe observation rather than ownership.


    

      
What does this person actually do on a project?

      
The single most important question — ask it early

      

        

          
STRONG ANSWER

          
"I am a primary electrical design engineer. My main output is the single line diagram and all the documentation that flows from it — equipment specifications, earthing calculations, load flow and short circuit studies. I join projects at FEED stage and my deliverables are what the secondary and PAC teams build from."

        

        

          
WEAK ANSWER

          
"I have experience in electrical engineering across various projects. I work on HV systems, substations, protection, and SCADA. I have been involved in design as well as commissioning activities and I am comfortable with a range of software tools."

        

      

    


    

      
Walk me through the last SLD you produced from scratch

      
Primary electrical — the single best qualifying question

      

        

          
STRONG ANSWER

          
"It was a 150kV to 33kV substation for a wind farm connection project. Double busbar configuration with six feeders. I produced it in AutoCAD from the basis of design document and it went through three review cycles before IFC. The busbar configuration was specified double because the client needed N-1 security of supply."

        

        

          
WEAK ANSWER

          
"I have worked on SLDs extensively. On my last project we had a 132kV substation and I was involved in reviewing and updating the SLD as the design progressed. The lead engineer was responsible for the original design."

Secondary electrical — separates design from review

      

        

          
STRONG ANSWER

          
"On the offshore substation project the I/O list had around 1,400 signals. The majority were digital inputs — status signals from circuit breakers and disconnectors. We had about 200 analogue signals for metering and around 80 digital outputs for remote control commands. I managed it in EPLAN and generated the terminal schedules directly from the same database."

        

        

          
WEAK ANSWER

          
"I have worked with I/O lists on various projects. They are quite complex documents with many different signal types. I have experience reviewing them and making sure the information is correct and consistent with the overall design."

STRONG ANSWER

WEAK ANSWER

          
"I have experience with protection systems from Siemens and ABB. I understand how protection works and I have been on projects where relay configuration was part of the scope. I am familiar with the general principles of distance and differential protection."

STRONG ANSWER

          
"Primavera P6. On one project the transformer delivery slipped by six weeks due to a manufacturing delay. The transformer was on the critical path for the electrical installation sequence. I reprogrammed the schedule to pull forward the civil and structural work that had float, and we recovered four of the six weeks without impacting the overall completion date."

        

        

          
WEAK ANSWER

          
"I have used both Primavera and MS Project depending on the project. Critical path is an important concept in project management. When delays occur the project team works together to find solutions and recover the programme where possible."

        

      

    


    

      
What went wrong on your last project and how did you handle it?

      
Universal — use on every call, every discipline, every level

      

        

          
STRONG ANSWER

          
"We issued a cable schedule at IFR with an error in the segregation zone assignments for the control cables in the switchgear building. It was caught during the client's interdisciplinary review. I owned it, set up a correction register, reissued within three days, and put a cross-discipline check step into our standard review procedure so it would not happen again."

        

        

          
WEAK ANSWER

          
"All projects have challenges. In my experience the key is good communication within the team and escalating issues to management when needed. I try to stay calm and work through problems systematically."

        

      

      
Strong engineers have stories. They remember a specific problem with specific details and describe what they personally did to fix it. Weak engineers give process descriptions. The answer tells you more about a candidate than their entire CV.
